# Finding the week's three items

The post lives or dies on whether the audience finds these three things worth knowing. Generic industry news they already saw makes the whole post pointless, however well written.

## Define the window

"This week" is the seven days ending today. Check today's date from the system - do not rely on memory, and do not assume the current year.

An item published up to ten days ago is acceptable if it only became relevant this week (a filing that got noticed late, a number that turned out to matter once something else happened). Anything older needs an explicit reason to be there, stated in the post.

## Search

Work from the topics, beats and named sources in `profile/audience.md`. Run several searches rather than one - each frame surfaces a different slice:

- the beat plus the current month and year
- the beat plus a specific event word: "announced", "filed", "ruling", "raised", "shut down", "report", "data"
- named companies, regulators or institutions from the audience profile
- the trade press the audience actually reads, searched directly
- the contrarian frame: what went wrong, what was quietly reversed, what didn't work

Look past the front page. The three items everyone posted about on Tuesday are the three items the audience already scrolled past. An item from a trade publication, a regulator's own site, an earnings call, a research paper or a primary filing is usually more valuable than the same story as covered by a general outlet.

Aim for eight to twelve candidates before choosing. Selection is where the quality comes from.

## Verify

Open every source. Do not write from a search snippet: snippets misstate dates, round numbers wrongly, and sometimes summarise a different article than the one they link to. If a page cannot be opened, either find the story at another outlet and open that, or drop the item.

For each item you keep, be able to state:

- the outlet
- the publication date
- one concrete fact - a number, a name, a decision, a date
- what changed, in one sentence
- why this audience specifically should care

If the last two are hard to write, the item is filler. Drop it.

## Choose

Prefer items that:

- the audience plausibly has not seen
- carry a hard detail worth repeating
- have a consequence, not just an announcement
- are not three variations on the same story

Prefer variety of type across the three - a regulatory or policy move, a company or market event, and a data point or research finding makes a fuller week than three funding rounds.

Actively include the unglamorous. A rule change with a compliance deadline is more useful to a practitioner audience than another product launch, and picking it signals that the writer reads past the headlines.

## Handle a thin week

Some weeks genuinely have less. Do not pad with a think-piece that contains no news, and do not stretch an old story. Options, in order of preference: widen the beat to an adjacent area the audience cares about; use a data release or a report rather than an event; say plainly in the post that it was a quiet week and that one of the three is a slow-burning story rather than news. Honesty about a quiet week reads as human. Manufactured significance does not.
