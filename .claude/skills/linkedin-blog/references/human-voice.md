# Writing so it reads as human

LinkedIn readers in 2025-2026 encounter AI-written posts constantly and have learned the pattern. The tells below are not stylistic preferences - they are the specific signals that make a reader think "this was generated," at which point they stop reading and quietly downgrade their opinion of the author. Avoiding them matters more than any other quality in this skill's output.

The underlying reason all of these read as machine-written is the same: they are the *average* choice. Language models converge on the most typical phrasing, the most balanced structure, the most agreeable stance. Human writing is idiosyncratic - it over-uses one favourite word, leaves a thought slightly unfinished, commits to an opinion without covering the counterargument, and includes a detail that serves no rhetorical purpose except that it actually happened.

> **Read the voice profile first.** Everything below describes defaults for a
> writer whose habits are unknown. The moment `profile/voice-profile.md` says
> otherwise, it overrides this file. Real people use em dashes. Real people use
> emoji as section headers, write numbered lists, and end with a question to
> their readers. When those appear in the user's own posts they are the voice,
> and sanding them off is the failure this skill exists to prevent - just in the
> opposite direction from the one this file is mostly about. Use this file to
> catch machine habits creeping into a voice, not to normalise a voice.

## 1. Building the voice fingerprint

When calibrating from the user's past posts, do not summarise them impressionistically ("professional but approachable"). That produces nothing usable. Extract observable, repeatable features:

- **Length**: typical character count, and the range.
- **Sentence rhythm**: average sentence length, and whether they mix very short sentences into longer ones or write uniformly.
- **Paragraph shape**: do they write paragraphs of 3-4 sentences, or single-sentence lines with blank lines between? The second is the "broetry" format - some people use it, many find it grating. Copy whatever they actually do.
- **Opening move**: how do the first 200 characters work? A claim, a question, a scene, a number, a piece of news? The first ~200 characters are what shows before "see more," so this is the highest-leverage habit to match.
- **Closing move**: a question to the reader, a flat statement, a call to action, a joke, nothing at all?
- **Person and stance**: I, we, or impersonal? Do they make claims or report neutrally? Do they name companies and people, or stay abstract?
- **Formality and jargon**: which technical terms do they use unglossed? Which everyday words do they prefer? Contractions or not?
- **Furniture**: emoji (which ones, where), hashtags (how many, which), bullet characters, ALL CAPS for emphasis, line-break patterns.
- **Fingerprints**: any phrase, construction or transition that appears in more than one post. These are gold - they are what makes the voice recognisably theirs.
- **Non-native or regional English**: if the user's English carries consistent patterns from another language, preserve them. Smoothing them into standard American business English is one of the most obvious ways a ghostwritten post stops sounding like its author.

Write these down as concrete notes with quoted examples from the samples. `profile/voice-profile.md` has the structure.

## 2. Tells at the character level

**Em dashes.** The single most recognised tell right now. Do not use them. Use a period, a comma, parentheses, or a colon. This applies even when an em dash would be correct - the association is strong enough that correctness doesn't help. Exception: if the user's own samples use em dashes, they are part of the voice and should stay.

**Curly quotes and typographic ellipses** mixed into otherwise plain text suggest generated output. Use straight quotes and three periods.

**Emoji as bullet points** (checkmarks, rockets, light bulbs, fire, pointing fingers as list markers) is a strong generated-content signal on LinkedIn unless the user genuinely writes that way. Follow the samples.

## 3. Tells at the sentence level

**The negation-reversal.** "It's not about X. It's about Y." / "This isn't just a Z - it's a W." Models reach for this constantly. It is now instantly recognisable. Say the thing directly.

**The rule of three, everywhere.** "Faster, cheaper, and more reliable." Three-item lists are fine occasionally; the tell is when every list has exactly three balanced items. Use two. Use four. Use one, and let it carry.

**Uniform sentence length.** Generated prose tends toward sentences of similar, medium length. Real writing varies hard - a nine-word sentence next to a thirty-five-word one that runs on slightly longer than it should because the writer was thinking as they typed.

**The dramatic one-line paragraph** used as a beat: "And that changes everything." Fine once in a post from someone who writes that way. A tell when it appears two or three times.

**Rhetorical question stacking.** "What does this mean for you? How should you respond? Where do you even start?" One question is a device. Three is a template.

**Perfectly parallel structure** across paragraphs - each of the three items introduced identically, each the same length, each ending on a similar note. Real writers give more room to whichever item they actually care about.

## 4. Tells at the structure level

**The hook-body-lesson-CTA skeleton.** Punchy opener, three tidy points, a lesson, "What's your take?" This is the shape of a million generated posts. A mini-blog can be shaped like an argument, a story, a correction of a common belief, or a report from somewhere the reader isn't - anything but the skeleton.

**Signposting.** "Here's the thing:" / "Let me explain." / "But here's what's interesting:" / "The bottom line?" / "Let that sink in." Delete on sight. Real writers just say the next thing.

**Recap closings.** Restating the three points in the final paragraph. Nobody who wrote 250 words needs to summarise them.

**Hashtag blocks.** Five or more hashtags reads as reach-farming. Two or three at most, and only if the samples use them.

**Generic engagement bait.** "Thoughts?" / "Agree?" / "Drop a comment below." If the post earns a response it will get one. A question is fine when it is a real question the user actually wants answered.

## 5. Tells at the word level

Words and phrases that have become markers of generated text. Avoid unless the user's samples use them:

*delve, leverage (as a verb), robust, seamless, seamlessly, game-changer, game-changing, unlock, harness, elevate, empower, foster, navigate the complexities, in today's fast-paced world, in an era of, the landscape is shifting, at the end of the day, moving forward, that said, it's worth noting, key takeaway, deep dive, double-edged sword, tapestry, testament to, pivotal, crucial, underscore, boasts, showcasing, transformative, revolutionary, cutting-edge, best-in-class, holistic, synergy, actionable insights, paradigm shift, the rise of, a stark reminder, speaks volumes, resonate, meticulous, realm, myriad, plethora*

Also avoid the hedging cluster that makes generated text feel weightless: *arguably, potentially, it could be argued, some might say, in many ways, to a certain extent.* Say what you think, or don't raise it.

The list is a guide to a category, not a blocklist to route around. The category is: vocabulary that sounds like business writing in general rather than like one specific person. When a word feels like it could appear in any post on any topic, replace it with something only this post would contain.

## 6. Tells at the stance level

This is subtler than vocabulary and matters more.

**Balance where a person would have a view.** Generated commentary presents both sides, then concludes that the truth lies somewhere in between and time will tell. Real posts take a position. Pick the one the evidence and the user's known views support, and say it.

**No stakes.** Nothing in a generated post costs the author anything. A human post contains something the author could be wrong about, something they were surprised by, or something mildly unflattering to their own industry or their own past opinion.

**Over-explanation.** Explaining what an obvious term means, or why the reader should care about something they already care about. Trust the audience defined in `profile/audience.md` - they are practitioners, not newcomers.

**Uniform enthusiasm.** Every item framed as exciting. Some news is bad, some is boring but consequential, some is overhyped. Saying so is one of the fastest ways to sound like a person.

**Manufactured intimacy.** "I was talking to a founder last week who told me..." Never write this unless the user told you it happened. Fabricated anecdote is the worst failure this skill can produce: it is a lie published under the user's name, and if challenged they cannot defend it.

## 7. What to do instead

- **Be specific to the point of being slightly odd.** Not "a major bank," but the bank's name. Not "significant growth," but the number and the period. Specifics are hard to generate and easy for a human who read the source.
- **Let one item get more space than the others.** Whichever the user would actually care about most.
- **Include one detail that serves no argumentative purpose** - a piece of context, an aside, something the writer noticed. Purposeless detail is a strong human signal because generated text is relentlessly on-task.
- **Allow imperfection.** A sentence fragment. A parenthetical that goes on a bit. Starting a sentence with "And" or "But." Repeating a word deliberately.
- **Say "I don't know" where it's true.** "I can't tell yet whether this matters" is credible in a way that confident synthesis is not.
- **Use the user's own recurring phrases** from the fingerprint. This does more for recognisability than anything else in this file.

## 8. Pre-delivery checklist

Run every item. Rewrite where it fails - do not rationalise a pass.

Items 1 to 4 apply only where the voice profile is silent - if the samples use
the construction, it stays. Items 5 to 13 apply always.

1. No em dashes.
2. No "not X, it's Y" construction anywhere.
3. No signposting phrases ("here's the thing," "the bottom line," "let that sink in," "what's interesting is").
4. No word from the section 5 list survives.
5. Sentence lengths vary rather than running uniformly medium.
6. The three items are not given identical structure and identical length - whichever matters most gets more room.
7. The post contains at least three concrete specifics (name, number, date) that could only come from having read the sources.
8. Somewhere in the post there is a line the author is actually asserting, that a reader could argue with.
9. No first-person experience, conversation, client or feeling that the user did not actually report.
10. Any closing question is a real one on a contested point, not "Thoughts?" or "Drop a comment below."
11. Emoji, hashtags, section headers, list markers and line-break style match the samples - including when the answer is "none," and including when the answer is "yes, quite a few."
12. Length is within the range in the voice profile.
13. Read against two real samples: an outside reader could not pick out the drafted one. Check this in both directions - too polished and too generic are the usual failures, but so is a post that avoids the author's own habits.
